## Hello, and thank you for visiting my portfolio. 
My name is Leigh, and I bring a unique blend of skills in UI design, software development, and a deep passion for educational technology. Over the years, I've dedicated myself to creating innovative learning experiences that seamlessly integrate technology with pedagogy.

In this portfolio, you'll find a showcase of my work that spans various projects, each reflecting my commitment to enhancing education through thoughtful design and cutting-edge technology. From user interfaces that prioritize accessibility and engagement, to software solutions that drive educational outcomes, my goal is to transform the learning landscape.

# Lenses
I have travelled quite a bit. My life has been an unorthodox exploration, born to parents from the Christian American Midwest—the granddaughter of a minister—but growing up in multicultural Southeast Asia (Hong Kong, Indonesia, and Singapore).

I returned to the US for college and attended The Savannah College of Art and Design, graduating Magna Cum Laude in both Art History and Painting. Originally I wanted to be an artist, but then realized I had a lot of student debt I needed to pay off, so starving artist wasn't in the cards for me. Art school taught me two things: how to teach myself and how to be curious.

After college I moved to DC and became a graphic designer though my roommate's company. She challenged me to learn Quark (the leading print production software at the time) and I got the job by reading the 4-inch thick manual cover-to-cover. My career spans 3 decades and I have worn many hats: design, JavaScript developer, UXUI researcher, Project Manager, and Product Manager. I have taught both coding and design for companies like LinkedIn Learning and Design Lab.

I have always been a teacher. It is in my heart. However, I did not pursue the traditional route of becoming an art teacher. Unlike Linda Harley—a tough scottish art teach at SAS who not only taught me to see, but who also likely saved my life—I pursued painting and history to try and interpret the world around me. My father who was a computer salesman for companies like Wang and IBM, furthered my curiosity by bringing home a Mac when I was around 9 years old. It had a few painting and graphics programs and I was hooked.

## Visual Hierarchy and Structured Communication
Unlike most, I come to Instructional Design and LXD from the lense of a graphic designer. Early on, I learned to use visual hierarchy to structure documents for easy comprehension and translate words into graphics that either reinforced words or replaced them all together. Interpreting words and ideas into visuals seems to be my superpower. I mastered the art of clear, concise communication by designing and formatting thousands of books, reports, and presentations. I mastered a new language—visual hierarchy—and learned every facet of the software, leading me to first teach the tools and then create my own.

## What I Thought Instructional Design Was
As a designer, I often had to restructure the work of the author so that the finished work was clearly communicated a visual, unspoken story. Whether I was reorganizing chapters, building interactives for deeper understanding, or crafting layouts with primary, secondary, and tertiary meanings—this is what I always believed instructional designers did. However, it's not always that clear cut. Working in education, I have seen many interpretations of the ID role. As the recent designer on a multi-courses overhaul project, I found that many ID thought their job was "making presentations" and copying and pasting the authors verbose work. Having a full understanding of visual design, I often pushed my colleagues to step back, think through the structure and meaning of the content and look for areas that could improve the overall story telling. For instance, one graphic of the 5 phased workflow was the base of the program structure. This led to a mismatch of audience learning objectives with the course content. The graphic was important, but did not need to dictate the flow or content of the program. I asked my colleagues to consider "what would the program look like IF the process phases were not the structure?" They ultimately stuck with what was dictated to them and focussing in on presentation of the words and I moved on to another project that was not simply cut and pasting into a slide template.

## IPSLA: The brain-child of two zany teacher willing to try something new
My freshman year of high school was spent in Hong Kong, at HKIS. (Jeff, seeing as you have been to HK and Singapore, we may know some of the same teacher in the international community.) The first course I ever LOVED was IPSLA, a cross-discipline curriculum taught by the brilliant Doctors Chan and Penna. Intro to Physical Science and Language Arts was active and thought provoking. Penna passionately jumped up on desks well before Dead Poets Society. Chan captured the class room with color and sounds, meaning and purpose...not just periodic tables. As a young punk, teaching us about particle bumping into each other by forming an impromptu mosh pit wasn't class. I was education in motion.

For our final exam, we were learning chemistry and reading Romeo and Juliette. Our challenge was to test two chemicals to decide which was poison and which was a sleeping potion. Extra credit was given to those who wanted to write the scientific method through the lense of Romeo and Juliette. I was one of the few who jumped at the chance. Everyday rote learning bored me to tears. This was different. It made a difference. Thirty years later I am still talking about it. My scientific write up included a new ending for Romeo and Juliette, using each section as a plot: observation, hypothesis, experimentation and conclusion. I was inspired. Having told this story over many years, I will answer the one question everyone asks. Yes, the whole paper was written in poetic stanzas. No, it was not iambic pentameter (I'm not a genius).
